Orbitals inside your compass frame

Hydrogen orbitals from the real equations, set by the quantum numbers n, l and m. They sit inside your 32 rays at 11.25°, the square and the r = 4 and r = 5.7 circles. Drag to turn, scroll or pinch to zoom. The top view is your notebook page.

How to use this page

Quick start

  1. Pick an element in the periodic table. The 3D view, its label and the readout switch to it.
  2. Choose a view under Show, next to the 3D view.
  3. Set the orbital with n, l and m, or swap the electron for a muon or another particle.
  4. Drag to rotate, pinch or scroll to zoom. Top matches your notebook page.

Good to know

  • Energy units (below) change every energy on the page.
  • Every number has a label: Measured from experiment, Exact solved from the equations, Computed calculated with a known error, Rough guess a simple model.
  • Learn or Lab: Learn shows the essentials and guided missions; Lab adds the solvers, Monte Carlo and downloads.
  • Share and export (below the hardware switch): copy a link to the exact view, save a picture, or download the data.
  • Heavy modes (GPU grids, Monte Carlo) run on your graphics card. 256³ needs a desktop GPU. The Hardware switch below picks Light or Full for you, and the solver falls back to the main processor on devices without a suitable graphics card.
  • Simulations pause when you scroll away from them and pick up again when you come back.
  • Pause a long run any time; results so far stay on screen.

Monte Carlo electrons

Instead of a grid, this scatters hundreds of random snapshots ("walkers") of all the electrons at once, the way a path tracer fires random light rays. Each frame moves every walker a little, and the averages sharpen over time like a ray-traced image clearing up. Because every electron is tracked together, they can dodge each other, which the grid’s mean field cannot do.

    What extreme gravity does to elements

    Gravity works through pressure and heat. Inside planets and stars, gravity squeezes the material underneath it. That raises the pressure and the temperature, and those are what change an element's state. This tool shows that link: pressure at depth = density × gravity × depth.

    Hypergravity in the lab. ESA's Large Diameter Centrifuge spins experiments at up to 20 times Earth's gravity for weeks or months. Researchers have used it to study how molten metals such as titanium aluminide solidify under high gravity. Gravity changes how the melt flows and settles, and so the alloy's structure, not which element it is.

    Neutron stars. Gravity is strong enough to crush matter to nuclear density. Deeper into the crust, nuclei become increasingly neutron-rich and exotic, then neutrons start to leak out, and near the base nuclei merge into shapes nicknamed “nuclear pasta”. Simulations suggest the crust is the strongest material known, about ten billion times stronger than steel.

    Where heavy elements are made. About half the elements heavier than iron form by rapid neutron capture (the r-process). In 2019, astronomers identified freshly made strontium in the glow after the neutron-star merger GW170817. That was the first direct sighting of a heavy element forming in one of these extreme-gravity collisions.

    Sources: ESA Large Diameter Centrifuge pages; Caplan and Horowitz, “Astromaterial Science and Nuclear Pasta” (2017); Watson et al., Nature 574, 497 (2019).

      The quantum mechanics behind this page

      The Schrödinger equation. An electron in an atom is a wave, ψ. Solving Ĥψ = Eψ for one particle around a nucleus gives ψ = Rnl(r) · Ylm(θ, φ). The radial part sets the size and the rings (radial nodes), and the angular part sets the lobes. The 3D view draws exactly these solutions.

      Four quantum numbers. n is the shell, l the shape (s, p, d, f), m the orientation, and spin s = ±½. The Pauli exclusion principle allows only one electron per set of four, which is why a subshell holds 2(2l + 1) electrons and a shell holds 2n². That rule builds the whole periodic table.

      Probability, not orbits. |ψ|² gives the chance of finding the particle at each point; each dot in the cloud is one simulated measurement. The uncertainty principle means the particle has no exact path, only this spread.

      Energy and mass. For one particle of reduced mass μ around a nucleus of charge Z: En = −13.6 eV × (μ ⁄ me) × Z² ⁄ n², and the size scales as (me ⁄ μ) ⁄ Z. Swap the electron for a muon and the atom shrinks about 200 times while its energy grows 200 times.

      Down the scale. Atom about 10⁻¹⁰ m → nucleus about 10⁻¹⁴ m → proton about 10⁻¹⁵ m → quarks and electrons, which show no size at all down to at least 10⁻¹⁸ m. The strong force (gluons) holds quarks inside nucleons, and its leftover reach binds nucleons into nuclei. The weak force (W and Z bosons) lets quarks change type, which is beta decay.

      Where this simple picture stops. In heavy atoms, inner electrons move at a large fraction of light speed, so relativity (the Dirac equation) shifts their energies; it is why gold is yellow and mercury is liquid. Quantum electrodynamics adds tiny corrections such as the Lamb shift. These are not included here.

      The math is the exact hydrogen solution, ψ = Rnl(r) · Ylm(θ, φ), using real spherical harmonics and Laguerre polynomials. Each dot is one simulated position measurement. Teal and coral mark the wave's sign, not charge. The cloud is scaled so 99.5% of the probability fits inside your r = 5.7 circle, and the panel shows what your r = 4 corresponds to in Bohr radii (a₀ ≈ 0.053 nm).

      Rendering runs on the GPU: orbital shapes, dot clouds and the volume view are computed per vertex, per point and per pixel in shaders, and the nucleus jiggle runs in a vertex shader. Changing n, l or m only updates a few numbers sent to the graphics card.

      Atoms with more than one electron have the same shapes but different sizes and energies. Shells mode estimates those sizes with Slater’s rules. The nucleus at the centre is enlarged thousands of times: a real nucleus is about 100,000 times smaller than its atom.
